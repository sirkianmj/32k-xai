Guide

A plain-English walkthrough for researchers. No programming required.

What is this platform?

This is a simulated society. It contains thousands of virtual people (“agents”), each one with its own personality, mood, and decision rules. The agents play economic games with each other — offering money, sending trust, deciding whether to reject unfair offers — and over time their choices produce patterns like cooperation, inequality, and trust.

The purpose is to study why those patterns emerge. Unlike a neural network, every single decision in this platform can be traced back to a specific number inside the agent — for instance, how much the agent values fairness, or how much distress it is currently feeling. You can point at one agent, at one moment, and ask “what made you do that?”, and get a real answer.

The simulation is calibrated against five human benchmarks — real data from published economic-game studies. When the platform says “PASS”, it means the simulated agents behaved within one standard error of what real humans did.

Quick start — 5 steps
  1. Open Scenarios. You will see eight pre-built experiments.
  2. Click any scenario. A settings page appears with three numbers (agents, ticks, seed). Leave them as they are.
  3. Click Run simulation. In a few seconds you will see a bar chart and five result cards.
  4. Read the results. Green bars = the agents matched human behaviour. Red bars = they did not. Click the “i” next to any benchmark for an explanation.
  5. Trace a decision. Click the blue “Trace a decision” button. Slide the agent and tick sliders to any position, then click “Show me the trace” to see every number behind one specific decision.
Tip. If you just want to see the platform work, open Scenarios → Baseline and click Run simulation. That takes the fewest steps.
Reading the results

Each run produces five benchmarks. These are the same five numbers that psychologists have measured in real human laboratories, so you can compare the simulation directly to people.

Ultimatum game — offer

Two strangers have £10 to split. One proposes a split; the other either accepts (both keep the money) or rejects (both get nothing). This benchmark is the average amount offered, as a fraction of the total. Human average: 0.40.

Ultimatum game — rejection

The fraction of offers that get rejected. Low offers are rejected more often than fair ones. Human average: 0.16.

Trust game — send

One person sends some money to a stranger, knowing the stranger will receive triple the amount. The sender is trusting the trustee to send something back. This benchmark is the average amount sent as a fraction of the sender's endowment. Human average: 0.502.

Trust game — return

The fraction of the received amount the trustee sends back to the sender. Human average: 0.372.

Wealth inequality (Gini)

A single number from 0 (everyone equal) to 1 (one person owns everything) that summarises how unequal wealth is after the simulation ends. Human comparison is to real wealth distributions, around 0.40.

What "PASS" means

Each benchmark has a human target and a standard error (SE). PASS means the simulated value is within one SE of the human target. SE captures how much the human data itself varies between studies. So PASS means: the simulation behaves consistently with the range of human findings.

Reading the decision trace

The trace shows every internal number for one agent at one moment. There are 56 fields in total, grouped by role. The most important groups:

Decision at this tick

What the agent chose to do: engage or avoid, what offer to make, what amount to send, whether to accept, how much to return.

Beliefs and values

The agent's internal estimates at the moment of choosing. The Q_MF values are habits learned from recent experience. The Q_MB values are forecasts based on a model of how the world works. The Q_net values blend the two based on the arbitration weight w_mb_eff. When w_mb_eff is small, the agent is running on habit; when it is large, it is planning.

Why it accepted or rejected

The Fehr-Schmidt utility (U_accept) is the number that decides whether to accept an offer. It is the agent's payoff minus a penalty for the offer being unfair. Two penalties are possible: inequity_neg (the partner has more) and inequity_pos (the agent has more).

Click any label in the trace to see its definition in plain English.

Building your own experiment

The Design experiments tab lets you build a custom study by combining four pieces:

  1. Population psychology — shift the mean or variance of any trait (e.g. make everyone slightly more neurotic). Useful when you want to test how population characteristics change behaviour.
  2. Interventions — add shocks to the simulation. There are four types:
    • Resource scarcity — reduce the money available in each interaction.
    • Distress injection — raise baseline stress levels for a subset of agents.
    • Social isolation — reduce the number of neighbours an agent can interact with.
    • Wealth redistribution — pull wealth toward the mean (like a tax-and-transfer policy).
  3. Targeting — an intervention can apply to everyone, to a random percentage, or to a subgroup defined by a trait (e.g. the top 30% on neuroticism).
  4. Measurement — how many agents, how many ticks, and how many seeds to average over.

When you click Run experiment, the platform produces a treatment-vs-control table (comparing targeted agents to non-targeted ones) and time-series charts (showing how behaviour evolves tick by tick).

Statistical terms

Standard Error (SE)

How much a measured value varies between independent studies. If a human average is 0.40 with SE 0.05, that means different published studies report values roughly between 0.35 and 0.45. PASS means the simulation falls in that range.

Delta (Δ)

The difference between the simulated value and the human target. Positive delta means the simulation scored higher than humans; negative means lower.

z-score (delta ÷ SE)

How many standard errors away from the human target the simulation is. A z of 0 means perfect agreement. A z of 1 is at the edge of the tolerance band. Below 1 is PASS.

Confidence interval (CI)

A range within which the true value is likely to fall. In this platform, the shaded band on the bar chart (± 1 SE) is a rough 68% interval.

p-value

How likely the observed result would be if there were no real effect. Small p means strong evidence. Not shown on the main dashboard, but available in the calibration log.

Seed

A number that makes random processes repeatable. Same seed + same settings = same result, exactly. Changing the seed gives a different random draw from the same population.

Glossary of engine terms

Agent

One virtual person in the simulation. The default is 8,000 agents; full scale is 32,000.

Tick

One round of the simulation. In each tick, every agent chooses a partner, decides whether to engage, plays both games, and updates its internal estimates.

Q-value

The agent's estimate of how much reward it will get from a given action. Higher Q = the agent expects this action to be more rewarding.

Model-based control (Q_MB)

The agent's plan. It simulates what would happen if it took each action, using a model of how the environment transitions between states.

Model-free control (Q_MF)

The agent's habit. It just remembers what worked before and does it again.

Arbitration weight (w_mb_eff)

The blend between habit and plan. Near 0 = mostly habit. Near 1 = mostly plan. Distress reduces this weight, forcing a reversion to habit.

Softmax

The formula that turns Q-values into probabilities. Actions with higher Q are more likely, but with some randomness.

Fehr-Schmidt utility

A utility function from behavioural economics that adds a “fairness penalty” to the agent's payoff. An unfair offer feels worse than the money alone would suggest.

Distress

An internal state that rises when the agent's offers are rejected and slowly decays. High distress reduces planning and biases the agent toward safer, more habitual choices.

Inverse temperature (τ)

How sharply the agent prefers higher-Q actions. High τ = strongly prefers the best option. Low τ = chooses more randomly.

About this platform

A research tool for causal, individual-level interrogation of emergent social behaviour.

What it is

32k-XAI is a research platform that simulates 32,000 cognitive agents. Each agent has a transparent, mathematically explicit brain — a small set of equations whose parameters come from real human psychometric data.

No neural networks. No large language models. Nothing hidden. Every decision can be traced back to a named parameter with a documented source.

What you can ask it

  • What happens to cooperation when resources become scarce?
  • Does psychological distress flatten or sharpen economic inequality?
  • How does social isolation change trust and reciprocity?
  • For a specific agent at a specific tick: why did it make the choice it made?

The science behind it

The platform reproduces five published human benchmarks within their standard errors: ultimatum offer rate, ultimatum rejection rate, trust-game send rate, trust-game return rate, and wealth Gini. All at full 32,000-agent scale.

This dashboard shows you those numbers live, along with the individual decisions behind them.
